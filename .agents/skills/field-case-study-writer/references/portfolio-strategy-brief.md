# Design Engineer Portfolio Strategy Brief

## Executive summary

Your portfolio should position you as a **frontend-leaning Design Engineer with senior product design judgment** — someone who can define product quality, build polished interfaces in production, think in systems, and contribute across product UI, design infrastructure, marketing surfaces, and interactive brand moments.

The portfolio should **not** behave like a conventional product designer portfolio, frontend engineer portfolio, creative developer portfolio, or marketing designer portfolio. The strongest direction is the overlap: clear product judgment, real frontend implementation, system thinking, thoughtful visual craft, accessibility, motion restraint, and clear tradeoff communication.

The hard recommendation is:

**Build a clarity-first, systems-led portfolio with a premium command-center editorial wrapper.**

Use the NASA / civic infrastructure / Halo / 80s computing / mission-control direction as an art-direction layer, not as a navigation gimmick. The portfolio should feel sharp, premium, operational, and trustworthy — not like a gamer HUD, vaporwave site, cyberpunk dashboard, or generic SaaS template.

A useful operating ratio:

**70% hiring clarity, 30% visual worldbuilding.**

The visual world should support the hiring story, never compete with it.

---

## Recommended positioning statement

Use a line close to this:

> **Nick Richardson is a Design Engineer with deep product-design roots. He designs high-quality software experiences and builds them in React, Next.js, TypeScript, and Tailwind — with a focus on systems, polish, accessibility, and customer-facing quality.**

Shorter hero version:

> **Frontend-leaning Design Engineer with senior product design judgment. I design thoughtful software and build it to production quality.**

The positioning should make it clear that you are not only a product designer, not only a frontend developer, and not only a visual designer. You are a hybrid operator who can move between product thinking, interface design, systems, implementation, and polish.

## Target audience and hiring signal

The audience is narrow:

- Design leaders and founders at smaller product companies
- Hiring managers for Design Engineer, Product Design Engineer, Design Systems, or craft-heavy Product Engineer roles
- Recruiters doing the first screen
- Frontend leads or staff engineers evaluating whether the work is production-minded
- Product-minded teams that value craft, quality, and customer care

Within the first ten seconds, the portfolio should communicate:

1. Who you are
2. What role you want
3. What makes you different
4. What quality of work you do
5. Where to click next

The user should immediately understand:

> Nick is a frontend-leaning Design Engineer who combines long-run product design judgment with production-ready frontend craft to build polished, system-driven software.

## What great Design Engineer portfolios have in common

Strong Design Engineer portfolios tend to show:

- A clear hybrid identity: design judgment plus production implementation
- Work that moves beyond mockups into shipped or buildable interfaces
- Strong systems thinking: components, tokens, primitives, documentation, architecture, governance
- Evidence of frontend craft: React, TypeScript, modern CSS, responsive behavior, performance, accessibility
- Interaction and motion quality without turning the site into a spectacle
- Clear role framing: what the person owned, what the team owned, what constraints shaped the work
- Concise case studies with visual proof
- Technical notes that are useful but not overwhelming
- A clear split between professional work and exploratory/lab work
- A homepage that gets to the work quickly

The key distinction:

A product designer portfolio often proves problem framing and UX process.  
A frontend engineer portfolio often proves implementation and technical depth.  
A creative developer portfolio often proves expressive interaction or visual experimentation.  
A Design Engineer portfolio must prove the bridge: **taste, systems, product judgment, frontend implementation, and tradeoff clarity.**

---

## What recruiters and hiring managers seem to value

Current Design Engineer roles tend to reward:

### Craft that survives implementation

Show that you can deliver polished UI in real code, not just pretty Figma frames. Include loading states, empty states, edge cases, accessibility, responsive behavior, and interaction details.

### Systems thinking

Show components, tokens, component APIs, documentation, design-to-code alignment, Storybook-style thinking, and reusable primitives.

### Autonomy and judgment

Hiring managers want to see that you can make decisions, understand constraints, communicate tradeoffs, and move work forward without waiting for perfect handoff conditions.

### Public proof

A portfolio, code samples, demos, shipped projects, or tooling work can all help prove that your work exists beyond presentation decks.

### Workflow leverage

Internal tools, better design-to-dev workflows, documentation, reusable assets, or AI-assisted workflows are increasingly valuable.

### AI fluency with human judgment

AI should be shown as a quality-aware workflow multiplier, not as a gimmick. Show where AI helped and where human judgment was essential.

## Recommended homepage structure

The homepage should be a fast, clear hiring surface.

Recommended structure:

1. **Hero**
   - Name
   - Positioning line
   - One-sentence value proposition
   - Two clear CTAs: `View work` and `Resume / Contact`

2. **Proof strip**
   - Example: `9 years product design • 2 years design engineering • React / Next.js / TypeScript / Tailwind • Systems • Accessibility • Interactive brand`

3. **Featured work**
   - Three primary projects
   - Each card should include project type, role, stack, year, and one sentence of value

4. **Capability band**
   - Product experiences
   - Frontend implementation
   - Systems/components/tokens
   - Marketing/brand surfaces
   - AI-assisted workflows

5. **Selected artifacts strip**
   - Interaction demos
   - System diagrams
   - Code snippets
   - Component snapshots

6. **Labs teaser**
   - One sentence
   - One link
   - Keep it secondary

7. **About / availability / footer**
   - Short bio
   - Location
   - Resume
   - Contact
   - GitHub / LinkedIn if relevant

The homepage should feel like a polished mission briefing: direct, structured, and confident.

## How to present confidential or hard-to-measure work

Treat confidentiality as a professionalism signal.

Use this approach:

- State confidentiality boundaries clearly
- Avoid exposing sensitive screens, numbers, names, or internal processes
- Use recreated/sanitized UI when appropriate
- Zoom into system fragments instead of full product surfaces
- Show diagrams, abstractions, components, and decision frameworks
- Explain your role and constraints clearly
- Offer more detail only in private walkthroughs if allowed

For hard-to-measure work, use proxy outcomes.

Strong proxy categories:

- System adoption
- Reduction in duplicated patterns
- Increased consistency
- Design-to-code parity
- Accessibility improvements
- Faster prototyping
- Improved team confidence
- Clearer governance
- Better documentation
- Reduced ambiguity
- Positive qualitative feedback
- Improved QA or review process

If you do not have metrics, say what you would measure next. That reads more senior than inventing weak numbers.

## How to frame design system work when outcomes are hard to quantify

Frame it as **quality infrastructure**.

Suggested structure:

1. The quality problem
2. The system intervention
3. The design/code alignment work
4. Examples of primitives, tokens, docs, or component APIs
5. Evidence of changed team behavior or product consistency
6. What the system unlocked next
7. What you would measure next

The goal is to show that design system work is not “making components.” It is creating shared infrastructure that helps teams ship coherent, accessible, high-quality product experiences.

## How to present older product design work without making it feel dated

Present older work as a **current retrospective**, not an archive dump.

Use:

- Freshly written overview in your current voice
- Carefully cropped visuals
- Modern diagrams that explain the system or workflow
- A “what I’d keep / what I’d change now” reflection
- Clear context about constraints at the time
- Emphasis on durable judgment over old visual style

Avoid:

- Showing too many dated screens
- Letting old UI style define your current taste
- Overexplaining process from years ago
- Pretending old work is newer than it is

The point is to show that your product judgment aged well, even if the visual layer belongs to an earlier period.

## Typography, layout, motion, and UI guidance

### Typography

Recommended direction:

- Primary sans: Geist, Inter, IBM Plex Sans, or similar
- Mono: IBM Plex Mono, JetBrains Mono, or Geist Mono
- Avoid too many typefaces
- Use mono for labels, metadata, code, and operational texture
- Use clean sans-serif typography for body and interface clarity

Optional editorial serif only if used sparingly and expertly. It is safer to skip it.

### Layout

Use:

- 12-column desktop grid
- Clear body text measure
- Consistent project cards
- Reusable metadata modules
- Strong spacing scale
- Dense but readable information panels
- No chaotic masonry layout

### Motion

Use:

- Small transitions
- Hover refinement
- Focus feedback
- Reveal motion
- Demo loops
- Physically believable easing
- Reduced-motion support

Avoid:

- Large theatrical page transitions
- Fake glitches
- Scanlines
- Animated chrome
- Motion that slows down reading
- Motion that competes with the work

### UI patterns

Good fits:

- Command palette
- Status bar as subtle metadata
- Project index
- Artifact drawer
- Keyboard shortcuts
- Copy-link affordances
- Filterable work grid if useful
- Colophon/build notes

Use only what supports clarity.
